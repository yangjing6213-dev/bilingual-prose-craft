# English Editing Cues

Use this reference as an editing lens, not an authorship test. A style habit is evidence about a passage, never proof of who wrote it.

## Judgment and strength

- The first five patterns are the clearest structural signals. One instance can justify editing when the wording truly adds no meaning.
- A dash, stacked hedge, routine hyphen, passive sentence, curly quote, or single piece of document narration is weak evidence by itself. Look for repetition, awkwardness, or a mismatch with the writer's sample.
- Every pattern has an ordinary use. Preserve real correction, evidence, limitations, deliberate rhythm, technical terms, and useful structure.
- Leave a watched phrase alone when it is inside an exact quotation, title, proper name, or passage that discusses the phrase instead of using it. Do not infer authorship from a writing date or from a cluster of cues.
- Read paragraphs as well as sentences. Repeated closers, openings, triads, or explanations may be visible only across the whole passage.

## A. Staging instead of stating

### 1. Negative contrast used for emphasis
**Look for:** “not X but Y,” “not only X but Y,” or the same contrast split across sentences.  
**Edit when:** The negative half answers no real misconception and the positive half merely restates the point. State the supported point directly.  
**Preserve when:** Both halves carry information or the negative half corrects a belief the reader might hold.  
**Example:** “It is not just a settings page; it is where the setting lives. Open the settings page.” → “Open the settings page.”

### 2. Repeated closers and dramatic fragments
**Look for:** A one-line paragraph that repeats what the preceding paragraph already showed, or a row of fragments used to amplify a point.  
**Edit when:** The line contributes no new fact, consequence, decision, or deliberate voice. Merge or remove it.  
**Preserve when:** A short sentence adds a real consequence or intentional emphasis.  
**Example:** “The export keeps a local copy. That is the real win.” → “The export keeps a local copy.”

### 3. Aphorisms and pseudo-depth
**Look for:** “At its core,” “the real question,” or a metaphor that sounds important but leaves the claim vague.  
**Edit when:** The source gives a concrete point that can replace the slogan. Do not guess what an unclear metaphor means.  
**Preserve when:** The metaphor is deliberate and the surrounding text explains it.  
**Example:** “Trust is the architecture of teamwork. Here, trust means reviewers can see each change.” → “Here, trust means reviewers can see each change.”

### 4. Staged run-ups
**Look for:** Standalone openers such as “Let’s dive in,” “Here’s what you need to know,” or “Honestly?” before a routine statement.  
**Edit when:** The opener announces a point instead of adding context. Remove the run-up, not merely its casual tone.  
**Preserve when:** A transition establishes sequence, a genuine warning, or a personal hesitation.  
**Example:** “Here is the key point: the server stores a draft.” → “The server stores a draft.”

### 5. Imaginary objections
**Look for:** “Don’t get me wrong,” “To be clear,” or a defense against an objection no one raised.  
**Edit when:** The setup has no specific reader concern and delays the actual claim.  
**Preserve when:** The text corrects a real misconception or answers a stated objection.  
**Example:** “To be clear, this is not meant to make uploads faster. The form accepts PDF files.” → “The form accepts PDF files.”

## B. Rhythm by formula

### 6. Forced groups of three
**Look for:** Three adjectives, examples, or claims added because three sounds complete.  
**Edit when:** Items repeat the same idea. Keep the distinct point or points.  
**Preserve when:** All three items carry independent meaning. Do not add or remove an item to satisfy a pattern.  
**Example:** “The tool helps teams plan, organize, and structure a schedule.” → “The tool helps teams plan a schedule.”

### 7. Repeated sentence openings
**Look for:** Several adjacent sentences that start with the same subject or phrase.  
**Edit when:** Repetition makes the passage mechanical. Vary or combine sentences without obscuring who did what.  
**Preserve when:** Parallel openings show deliberate comparison, sequence, or rhetorical rhythm.  
**Example:** “The team checked the draft. The team fixed two links. The team sent it.” → “The team checked the draft, fixed two links, and sent it.”

### 8. Em and en dashes as default connectors
**Look for:** Dashes joining clauses where the relation is left unstated.  
**English convention:** In an ordinary English rewrite, do not introduce em dashes or en dashes. If the writer's sample uses them, match its approximate rate. Prefer punctuation that states the relation.  
**Edit when:** A dash is habitual, unclear, or inconsistent with the author's sample. One dash alone is weak evidence of a style problem.  
**Preserve when:** It is part of an exact quotation, proper name, code, URL, command, or a requested house style.  
**Example:** “The editor checked the file — it is ready.” → “The editor checked the file, so it is ready.” Use “so” only if the source supports that relation; otherwise use a period.

### 9. Stacked qualifiers
**Look for:** Several hedges that express the same uncertainty.  
**Edit when:** Qualifiers duplicate one another. Keep each distinct condition, evidence limit, and scope boundary.  
**Preserve when:** The uncertainty is meaningful or required by the source. This cue is weak alone.  
**Example:** “It may possibly be able to work.” → “It may work.”

### 10. Hyphenated pairs everywhere
**Look for:** A hyphen added to ordinary phrases in every grammatical position.  
**Edit when:** The compound is awkward or a post-noun hyphen is unnecessary.  
**Preserve when:** A dictionary-standard, pre-noun, or technical compound needs its hyphen. This cue is weak alone.  
**Example:** “The report is high-quality.” → “The report is high quality.”

### 11. Passive voice or missing actors
**Look for:** A sentence that hides who acted or leaves out a useful subject.  
**Edit when:** The actor is known and naming it improves clarity.  
**Preserve when:** The actor is unknown, irrelevant, or less important than the result. Never invent an actor. This cue is weak alone.  
**Example:** “The schedule was changed by Mira.” → “Mira changed the schedule.”

## C. Inflation and borrowed authority

### 12. Stock AI vocabulary
**Look for:** Words such as “delve,” “crucial,” “robust,” or “seamless” used as generic praise.  
**Edit when:** A word is empty, repetitive, or less accurate than a concrete description.  
**Preserve when:** It is technically precise or matches the author's voice.

### 13. Inflated significance
**Look for:** Claims of a new era, legacy, milestone, or bright future without supporting evidence.  
**Edit when:** The importance is asserted but no concrete outcome supports it.  
**Preserve when:** A verifiable milestone, consequence, or attributed view is stated.

### 14. Vague connections
**Look for:** “Connected to,” “linked with,” or “associated with” where the actual relationship is unclear.  
**Edit when:** The source states a more precise relation. Use that relation.  
**Preserve when:** The source itself is uncertain. Do not guess whether the link is causal, organizational, temporal, or merely coincidental.

### 15. Empty participial add-ons
**Look for:** A trailing phrase such as “highlighting,” “underscoring,” or “showcasing” that repeats the main clause.  
**Edit when:** It adds no separate fact, reason, result, or purpose.  
**Preserve when:** It contributes evidence or a real consequence.

### 16. Sales language
**Look for:** Generic praise such as “powerful,” “game-changing,” or “best-in-class.”  
**Edit when:** The praise replaces useful facts. State the feature or outcome the source actually supports.  
**Preserve when:** It is a clearly attributed opinion or part of the requested promotional genre; do not invent metrics.

### 17. Borrowed authority
**Look for:** “Experts say,” prestige names, or publication lists standing in for an actual claim.  
**Edit when:** The source and claim are known; name them accurately. If not, preserve the attribution as unverified or flag the missing source.  
**Preserve when:** The authority is relevant and accurately represented. A missing citation alone is not a style tell.

### 18. Avoiding “is,” “are,” and “has”
**Look for:** Indirect wording that avoids a simple verb.  
**Edit when:** A direct sentence preserves the same amount, comparison, scope, and capability.  
**Preserve when:** The rewrite would turn possibility into completion or alter a comparison. “Can provide” is not “has provided.”

## D. Formatting by formula

### 19. Decorative bold
**Look for:** Bold applied to routine phrases or labels throughout a passage.  
**Edit when:** It does not help scanning or carry emphasis.  
**Preserve when:** It marks warnings, navigation, or requested formatting.

### 20. Decorative headings
**Look for:** Headings with repeated emoji, arrows, title-case slogans, or separators that add no structure.  
**Edit when:** The decoration obscures the section's subject.  
**Preserve when:** It is part of a template, brand, or requested style. Keep heading hierarchy unless asked to change it.

### 21. Curly quotation marks
**Look for:** Curly quotes where the writer or target format uses straight quotes.  
**Edit when:** The punctuation conflicts with the target convention.  
**Preserve when:** The quote is exact or curly quotes are the expected style. This cue is weak alone.

## E. Chat and drafting residue

### 22. Chatbot residue
**Look for:** Empty “Great question,” “I hope this helps,” or similar wrappers in standalone prose.  
**Edit when:** It contributes no useful politeness or relationship.  
**Preserve when:** It is a genuine greeting or sign-off in correspondence.

### 23. Knowledge-limit disclaimers and guesses
**Look for:** Repeated “I cannot verify” caveats, unsupported guesses, or stale knowledge disclaimers.  
**Edit when:** A caveat is duplicated or does not affect the reader's decision.  
**Preserve when:** The source limit, date, or uncertainty changes how a claim should be read. Never replace a guess with a fabricated fact.

### 24. Heading repeated in the opening sentence
**Look for:** The first sentence restating the heading without adding a point.  
**Edit when:** It is truly redundant.  
**Preserve when:** The opening sentence adds a needed definition, result, or scope.

### 25. Narrating the document or drafting process
**Look for:** “This section will discuss,” “the following table shows,” or an account of how the writer assembled the text.  
**Edit when:** The reader needs the subject, not a description of the page. This cue is weak alone.  
**Preserve when:** It is an instruction, changelog, source credit, methodological caveat, or essential navigation.

## F. Writing for the actual reader

### 26. Re-explaining context the reader already has
**Look for:** A reply that repeats the problem and diagnosis before reaching its answer.  
**Edit when:** The surrounding conversation shows the reader already knows that context. Lead with the decision and keep only reasoning that could change it.  
**Preserve when:** The text must stand alone or the surrounding exchange is unavailable. If unsure, do not remove context.
